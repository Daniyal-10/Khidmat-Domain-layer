# Humanitarian Business Reference Model (HBRM)

## Chapter 1 â€” The Humanitarian Ecosystem

Humanitarian assistance is delivered within a complex, interconnected ecosystem of actors, each with distinct roles, responsibilities, and relationships. Understanding this ecosystem requires mapping these actors as participants in a business process rather than as data entities.

The humanitarian ecosystem must conceptually distinguish between **Actors** and **Roles**:

- **Actors** are the enduring participants in the ecosystem (e.g., Governments, NGOs, Community Organisations, Donors, Individuals, Volunteers).
- **Roles** are the functional responsibilities that an Actor may perform (e.g., Implementing Organisation, Funding Organisation, Coordinating Organisation, Monitoring Organisation, Evaluating Organisation).

Crucially, one Actor may perform multiple Roles, and one Role may be performed by different Actor types. The following sections describe the primary participants through the lens of the Roles they typically assume:

**Affected Populations**
The individuals, households, and communities experiencing vulnerability or crisis. They are the primary focus of humanitarian assistance, but they are not merely passive recipients; they are active agents in their own recovery, possessing their own capacities, social networks, and decision-making authority over their lives.

**Host Governments**
The sovereign bodies bearing the primary responsibility for the protection, welfare, and recovery of populations within their jurisdiction. Governments set the regulatory frameworks, coordinate national response strategies, and frequently provide direct assistance through state-run safety nets, public services, and disaster management agencies.

**Implementing Organisations (NGOs and Agencies)**
These include International Non-Governmental Organisations (INGOs), National NGOs, United Nations agencies, and local civil society Organisations. They are the primary direct providers of humanitarian assistance, deploying staff, local expertise, and resources to identify needs and deliver services. Importantly, implementing Organisations are distinct from the specific programmes or coordination structures (such as sector clusters) they lead or participate in. An Organisation is an enduring entity; a programme is a structured, funded initiative.

**Donors and Funders**
Actors that provide financial, material, or technical support to other Organisations, enabling humanitarian work without necessarily delivering direct assistance themselves. Donors include institutional funders, philanthropic foundations, and private individuals. Their role introduces accountability requirements regarding how resources are allocated, utilized, and reported.

**Informal and Community-Based Actors**
A critical, often first-responding category of humanitarian actor operating outside formal institutional structures. This includes emergent mutual-aid groups, spontaneous volunteers, local self-help networks, and community or religious committees (such as local mosque funds). These actors operate with high proximity, speed, and local trust, making them legitimate humanitarian actors in their own right rather than temporary substitutes for formal Organisations.

Within specific cultural contexts, such as Islamic charitable giving, this formal/informal distinction is particularly pronounced. There is a structural difference between "vertical" philanthropy (formal, institutionalized Zakat Organisations) and "horizontal" philanthropy (informal, private Sadaqah given directly through community networks). Both function as vital business actors, yet they operate with different mechanisms of accountability, trust, and proximity.

The interactions among these actors form the foundation of humanitarian assistance. Understanding their respective roles is necessary before exploring the interests, tensions, and lifecycle processes that govern their collaboration.

## Chapter 2 â€” Stakeholder Interests and Tensions

The humanitarian ecosystem is shaped not only by the distinct roles of its actors but by the structural tensions between their respective interests. These tensions are not necessarily signs of dysfunction; rather, they reflect the complex realities of operating in crisis environments. Humanitarian Organisations must balance these competing pulls when making decisions, designing Programmes, and delivering assistance.

**Donor Accountability vs. Affected-Population Accountability**
A fundamental structural tension exists between accountability to those who fund humanitarian assistance and accountability to those who receive it. Donors require rigorous financial reporting, compliance with funding mandates, and measurable alignment with their strategic priorities. In contrast, affected populations require systems that provide a voice, responsive complaint and feedback mechanisms, and assistance that aligns with their actual, lived needs. Balancing the top-down requirements of institutional funders with the bottom-up legitimacy required by beneficiaries remains a continuous operational challenge.

**Speed vs. Verification and Data Quality**
During a crisis, there is an intense operational pressure to deliver rapid response and life-saving assistance. This speed inherently conflicts with the need to rigorously verify information, ensure data quality, and mitigate fraud. Humanitarian practice rarely resolves this by choosing one extreme over the other; instead, the tension is managed through accelerated minimum verification controls and community validation, prioritizing immediate safety while deferring deeper verification until stability allows.

**Donor-Driven Standardization vs. Locally-Led Legitimacy**
To ensure fairness, scale, and compliance, formal humanitarian architecture relies heavily on standardizationâ€”uniform eligibility criteria, structured beneficiary lists, and standardized financial controls. However, this standardization frequently conflicts with the flexibility, informal-network legitimacy, and nuanced local knowledge that make locally-led responses effective. Local Organisations and community-based actors are often caught in the tension of leveraging their informal legitimacy while being pressured to conform to external institutional standards to secure funding and recognition.

**Consent vs. Humanitarian Necessity**
In humanitarian operations, the ideal standard is to obtain genuine, informed consent before collecting personal data or intervening. However, in emergency contexts involving acute vulnerability, immediate security threats, or severe logistical constraints, obtaining meaningful consent is frequently infeasible. Sector practice resolves this tension not by abandoning consent, but through a bounded "necessity exception." Action may proceed without consent to preserve life or safety, but this exception is strictly provisional, never serving as a permanent substitute for returning agency to the affected individual once the immediate crisis has passed.

**Automation vs. Human Oversight**
As Organisations seek to increase scale and efficiency, there is a pull toward automating decisions and workflows. This efficiency interest is structurally in tension with the requirement for meaningful human oversight, particularly when decisions materially affect a person's rights, dignity, or access to essential services. Balancing the operational capacity provided by automation against the ethical necessity of human accountability is a recurring operational consideration.

## Chapter 3 â€” The Humanitarian Lifecycle (Business View)

Humanitarian assistance operates across at least two structurally distinct lifecycles running concurrently: a programme-level coordination cycle and a case-level individual cycle. 

The programme-level cycle governs how the humanitarian system plans and resources itself (needs assessment, strategic response planning, resource mobilization, and monitoring). It establishes the capacity, budget, and mandate for an Organisation's work. In contrast, the case-level lifecycle governs how a specific person or household's case unfolds within that capacity. This chapter focuses exclusively on the macro sequence of the case-level lifecycle, recognizing that it is constrained by the resources mobilized during the programme cycle.

**The Case-Level Lifecycle**
A humanitarian case generally moves through a recognized sequence of events. However, this sequence is structurally non-linear; it forms a continuous loop rather than a straight line. As circumstances change, new information routinely sends a case back to an earlier reassessment stage.

1. **Screening and Intake:** A need is first identified or a person presents themselves to the system. Basic identity and immediate vulnerability information are recorded to determine initial routing.
2. **Assessment:** The person's broader circumstances, relationships, and specific needs are explored in depth. Evidence is gathered to build a contextual understanding of their reality.
3. **Planning (Support Planning):** Based on the assessment, a specific intervention or set of interventions is selected and planned. Eligibility is determined through human review, matching the verified need against the available programme resources.
4. **Implementation (Support Delivery):** The planned assistanceâ€”whether financial, material, protective, or rehabilitativeâ€”is delivered to the affected person.
5. **Monitoring and Reassessment:** The situation is actively observed following the intervention. If needs have evolved, or if new vulnerabilities emerge, the case loops back to the Assessment or Planning stages. This accountability loop ensures that the assistance remains relevant to the person's changing reality.
6. **Closure:** When the specific needs addressed by the case plan have been met, or the person transitions out of the programme's scope, the case reaches formal closure.

**Engagement Status vs. Developmental Trajectory**
Throughout this lifecycle, a critical distinction is maintained between two separate measures of progress: the individual's administrative status within a specific Programme (their Engagement Stage) and their underlying progress toward resilience (their Human Development Stage). 

An individual may be actively enrolled and receiving services (an active Engagement Stage) while remaining in acute vulnerability (a crisis Development Stage). Conversely, they may graduate to self-reliance (a resilient Development Stage) and successfully exit the system (a closed Engagement Stage). These two trajectoriesâ€”administrative participation and actual human recoveryâ€”are evaluated and tracked independently.

**Post-Closure: Evaluation and Learning**
While the formal case-management lifecycle ends at closure, the humanitarian process relies on a separate but connected discipline of evaluation and Organisational learning. Measuring the long-term outcome of an intervention and assessing its broader impact are treated as programmatic responsibilities that consume the outputs of the case lifecycle, ensuring that the system continuously learns and improves its future assessments without keeping individual cases open indefinitely.

## Chapter 4 â€” Business Capabilities

To execute the lifecycle described in Chapter 3 and navigate the tensions outlined in Chapter 2, a humanitarian Organisation relies on a set of core business capabilities. These capabilities are defined by the outcomes they achieve rather than the specific individuals, departments, or software systems that execute them. 

**The Core Cognitive Lifecycle Capabilities**
A humanitarian response relies on a sequential cycle of sense-making and decision-making capabilities:
- **Evidence & Knowledge Acquisition:** The ability to continuously gather claims, observations, and data from affected populations and the operational environment.
- **Understanding Formation:** The ability to synthesize disparate pieces of evidence into a coherent, contextual understanding of a person's or community's reality.
- **Reasoning & Justified Recommendation:** The ability to evaluate needs against available resources and constraints to produce actionable, justifiable recommendations for assistance.
- **Responsible Action:** The ability to execute those recommendations safely and effectively, delivering the planned support to the intended recipient.

**Cross-Cutting Execution Capabilities**
Several capabilities support and regulate the core cognitive lifecycle across time and Organisational boundaries:
- **Verification:** The ability to independently confirm the accuracy of claims or observations before they are used as the basis for consequential decisions.
- **Continuity & Re-assessment:** The ability to maintain an ongoing relationship with an affected individual or household, updating the Organisation's understanding as the individual's situation changes over time.
- **Cross-Organisational Coordination:** The ability to align interventions, share context, and prevent duplication of effort with other actors in the humanitarian ecosystem. 

**Accountability and Learning Capabilities**
In addition to the capabilities required to manage individual cases, Organisations must maintain distinct capabilities dedicated to systemic accountability and learning:
- **Monitoring, Evaluation, Accountability, and Learning (MEAL):** A structured, independent capability dedicated to observing operations, evaluating outcomes against intended objectives, and integrating those lessons back into future programme design. MEAL operates on its own cadence and evaluates the health of the broader response, rather than functioning merely as the final step of a single case.
- **Complaints and Feedback Mechanisms (CFM):** A dedicated, structurally separate capability providing affected populations with a direct channel to raise grievances, report abuse, or provide feedback. Maintaining CFM as distinct from case management is critical for preserving its independence and ensuring that accountability flows upward from the community.

**Altitude Distinctness in Capabilities**
Crucially, several capabilities exist at two distinct altitudes: the programme (coordination) level and the individual case level. For example, "Needs Assessment" at the programme level involves evaluating the macro-vulnerabilities of an entire region to justify funding, whereas case-level "Needs Assessment" involves evaluating the specific, granular needs of one household to determine their eligibility for a service. A complete capability architecture must distinguish between these altitudes, as they serve different purposes and operate on different lifecycles.

## Chapter 5 â€” Business Services and Value Streams

Business capabilities, as defined in Chapter 4, do not exist in isolation. They are executed to produce concrete outputsâ€”business servicesâ€”which are then chained together to form value streams that deliver measurable outcomes to affected populations.

**Business Services as Capability Outputs**
A Business Service represents the externally consumable outcome produced through one or more business capabilities. This establishes the Khidmat AI modelling convention for defining how work is exchanged between domains.

For example, the capability of "Verification" produces the service of a *verified claim*. The capability of "Understanding Formation" produces an *assessed need*. The capability of "Reasoning" produces an *approved support plan*.

Defining these services establishes clear governance and ownership boundaries. As a beneficiary moves through the humanitarian lifecycle, they are moving through a sequence of these services. The handoff of a service (e.g., passing a verified claim to a case manager for support planning) represents a distinct operational milestone.

**The Dual-Flow Nature of Value Streams**
A humanitarian value stream is the end-to-end sequence of services that connects a triggering need to a resolved outcome. Crucially, these value streams comprise two concurrent, interdependent flows that humanitarian operations practically decouple:
1. **The Information and Knowledge Flow:** The process of gathering claims, verifying identity, assessing needs, and forming an understanding of the context.
2. **The Material and Resource Flow:** The physical procurement, logistics, and direct delivery of aid (e.g., cash, shelter, medical supplies).

By structuring the value stream this way, Organisations can run a dedicated "knowledge layer" (assessing who needs what) independently of the "logistics layer" (the physical delivery mechanism).

**Value Streams by Altitude**
Just as capabilities operate at different altitudes, value streams themselves split along a case versus programme axis:
- **Case-Level Value Streams:** These streams are triggered by an individual or household's registration and follow the sequential, looping lifecycle of assessment, planning, delivery, and closure for that specific case.
- **Programme-Level Value Streams:** These streams are triggered by aggregated, area-level events (e.g., a community-wide crisis response). They do not decompose cleanly into individual case-level capability sequences. Instead, they organize the broader coordination, resource mobilization, and structural response that ultimately dictate what services are available to individual cases.

A comprehensive humanitarian architecture must map and respect both types of value streams, ensuring that individual case outcomes are linked to the overarching programmatic goals that funded them.

## Chapter 6 â€” Intervention Categories

The humanitarian ecosystem delivers many forms of assistance, but these interventions are not simply a flat list of items. Instead, humanitarian interventions are structured along three fundamentally distinct, universally recognized dimensions. A specific intervention exists at the intersection of these dimensions.

**1. Sector/Domain (What need is addressed)**
This dimension categorizes interventions based on the area of human vulnerability they target. Common sectors include Food Security, Health, Education, Shelter, Protection, and Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH). Crucially, sector categorizations are typically used at the *programme altitude* to organize macro-level response, coordinate NGOs, and pool funding.

**2. Modality (How it is delivered)**
This dimension describes the delivery mechanism. The primary modalities are:
- **In-Kind Assistance:** Physical goods (e.g., tents, food parcels).
- **Service Delivery:** Direct actions (e.g., medical treatment, legal counseling).
- **Cash and Voucher Assistance (CVA):** Financial transfers providing recipients the flexibility to meet their own needs.

Modality decisions are typically made at the *case altitude*, based on local market feasibility, household appropriateness, and delivery constraints. For example, a "Shelter" need (Sector) might be met via cash to pay rent (Modality) or by delivering a tent (Modality).

**3. Temporal/Objective Phase (Why/When it is delivered)**
This dimension categorizes the intervention based on its strategic objective within the beneficiary's trajectory:
- **Emergency Relief:** Immediate, life-saving assistance aimed at stabilizing a crisis.
- **Rehabilitation/Recovery:** Restoring access to basic services and beginning the transition out of crisis.
- **Development/Resilience:** Long-term support aimed at establishing sustainable independence and preventing future crises.

Modern humanitarian practice recognizes that these phases are not strictly sequential; an individual or community may receive emergency relief and developmental support concurrently.

**Multi-Sectoral Interventions**
The mapping between a delivered intervention and a sector of need is frequently many-to-many. For instance, Multipurpose Cash (MPC) is a single modality that is designed to address multiple sector categories concurrently (e.g., Food, Shelter, and WASH). This reflects a business reality where integrated case management spans multiple capabilities rather than operating in strict sector silos.

**Business Concepts vs. Operational Implementations**
The three dimensions described aboveâ€”Sector, Modality, and Temporal Phaseâ€”are stable, enduring business concepts. Conversely, the specific items delivered (e.g., "jerry cans," "school fees," "agricultural seeds") are volatile operational implementations that vary significantly by region, Organisation, and emergency type. The business logic of humanitarian assistance relies on the stable dimensions, treating the specific operational catalogue as a downstream, highly contextual configuration.

## Chapter 7 â€” Operational Objectives and Success

The ultimate goal of humanitarian assistance is not the delivery of aid, but the restoration of human dignity, resilience, and independence. Operational success at the business level is defined by whether the system advances these outcomes. 

**Defining Success in Humanitarian Terms**
Success is achieved when a humanitarian system can demonstrate:
- **Dignity and Agency:** The affected population is treated as active participants in their recovery, with control over their own narrative and choices.
- **Verification Integrity:** Assistance is provided based on trustworthy, corroborated evidence rather than assumptions or unverified claims.
- **Timeliness:** Vulnerabilities are identified early, and interventions are delivered with speed appropriate to the level of crisis, without sacrificing necessary safety checks.
- **Non-Duplication and Continuity:** Interventions are coordinated across actors to avoid overlapping aid, and support follows the individual cohesively over time.
- **Long-Term Recovery:** Assistance addresses underlying vulnerabilities to build sustainable independence, rather than merely treating repeated symptoms.

**Indicators of Failure**
Conversely, the system fails when individuals become trapped in prolonged dependency, when cases are repeatedly closed and reopened without progress, when interventions conflict due to a lack of shared context, or when the system serves the administrative convenience of the Organisation over the lived reality of the beneficiary.

> **Open Discovery Assumption:** The specific, informal criteria the client and domain team currently use in practice to judge whether an intervention "worked" (prior to formal outcome indicators) remains an unresolved discovery topic.

## Chapter 8 â€” Governance and Authority Boundaries

Humanitarian assistance involves decisions of high consequence. Establishing clear boundaries regarding who has the authority to make, approve, or override these decisions is a core business requirement.

**Decision Altitudes and Authority**
- **Case-Level Authority:** Decisions regarding an individual's specific needs, eligibility, and support plan are governed by frontline workers (e.g., case managers, field officers). Their authority is bounded by the resources and criteria defined at the programme level.
- **Programme-Level Authority:** Decisions regarding budget allocation, target populations, intervention catalogues, and overarching response strategies are governed by programme directors or coordination bodies. They establish the operational rules that case workers execute.
- **Cross-Organisational Authority:** In a shared ecosystem, authority is distributed. No single Organisation can unilaterally alter another Organisation's independent assessment, but Organisations can securely share verified evidence to inform mutual understanding.

**The Threshold for Human Review**
A fundamental governance boundary exists between automated operational efficiency and ethical accountability: *Any decision whose error could materially affect a person's rights, safety, dignity, legal status, eligibility, access to essential services, or long-term wellbeing requires meaningful human review.* Automation may assist in gathering evidence and proposing recommendations, but the authority to execute high-consequence decisions remains strictly human.

> **Open Discovery Assumption:** Who, in actual humanitarian practice, possesses the specific operational authority to override a system-generated recommendation (and under what exact circumstances) remains an unresolved discovery topic.

## Chapter 9 â€” Boundary Conditions and Regional/Cultural Variation

While the core cognitive lifecycle and the fundamental business capabilities described in this reference model are intended to be universal, humanitarian assistance is deeply influenced by the context in which it operates. A robust architecture must explicitly delineate what is universal from what is culturally or regionally variable.

To accurately model humanitarian reality, the architecture explicitly distinguishes three conceptual layers of behaviour and policy:

### Universal Business Principles
Practices that remain constant across all humanitarian contexts. The requirement for evidence, the necessity of understanding context before acting, the separation of information flow from material delivery, and the imperative of human dignity are universal boundary conditions. They apply equally in an acute conflict zone or a long-term developmental context.

### Regional Business Practices
Practices influenced by geography, culture, regulation, or local humanitarian ecosystems. Specific operational mechanisms frequently vary:
- **Philanthropic Frameworks:** In contexts guided by Islamic charitable giving, assistance must respect the structural distinctions between "vertical" (formal, institutional Zakat) and "horizontal" (informal, community-based Sadaqah) mechanisms. The rules of eligibility and accountability vary significantly between these frameworks.
- **Community Structures:** The definition of a "household," the role of community elders, and the acceptable methods of verification vary widely across cultures. A universal system must treat these cultural definitions as legitimate contextual variables rather than exceptions to a rigid norm.

### Organisation-Specific Operational Policies
Internal policies established by individual organisations that influence how they execute humanitarian work while remaining compatible with broader humanitarian principles. This includes specific eligibility thresholds, internal approval chains, and proprietary risk matrices.
